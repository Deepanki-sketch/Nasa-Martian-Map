1. Challenge Mission Brief & Objectives

Over five decades of Mars exploration, NASA orbiters and robotic rovers have collected massive quantities of high-resolution elevation, thermal, spectral, and optical data. However, these datasets reside in separate scientific archives. Robotic rovers like Curiosity or Perseverance move at ~0.1 km/h and can stop for days to evaluate risks. Human astronauts cannot do this.

When the first crew lands on Mars, an Extravehicular Activity (EVA / "Marswalk") is strictly bounded by human physiology and spacesuit life-support consumables (typically 6 to 8 hours of oxygen, battery power, and water cooling). The objective of this challenge is to fuse NASA's multi-mission planetary data into an operational, layered tactical map that empowers human explorers to accomplish critical science while guaranteeing their return to safety.

Mandatory Challenge Criteria & Scoring Rules

  • Target Site & Science Goal: Select a specific site with an authentic scientific objective. The challenge explicitly highlights studying boxwork formations (such as those investigated by Curiosity at Mount Sharp in Gale Crater).
  • Multi-Mission Data Layering: Integrate at least three distinct types of planetary data:
    1. Topography & Elevation: MOLA laser altimetry or HiRISE stereo DTMs (slopes, hazards).
    2. Optical Imagery: CTX (6 m/px) or HiRISE (30 cm/px) visible surface mosaics.
    3. Compositional / Environmental: CRISM mineral spectra, THEMIS thermal inertia (rock vs. loose sand), or surface meteorology.
  • Feasible, Hazard-Aware Route Traversal: Compute viable paths avoiding dangerous terrain (slopes >15°, impassable boulder fields, sand traps) while hitting scientific sampling targets.
  • Human-Centric Life-Support Safety: Account for astronaut metabolic load, suit mobility constraints, communication line-of-sight (LOS) to the habitat, and emergency abort margins.

3. Science Target: Mount Sharp Boxwork Formations

The challenge specifically recommends focusing on the boxwork formations of Mount Sharp (Aeolis Mons) in Gale Crater (4.59° S, 137.44° E).

Why Boxwork is the Ideal Scientific & Survival Showcase

The Astrobiology Prize: Billions of years ago, long after Gale Crater's surface lakes evaporated, warm underground water percolated through bedrock fractures. Minerals dissolved in the water cemented the cracks. When harsh winds later eroded away the softer surrounding rock, a massive honeycomb of interconnected mineral ridges was left standing. These deep groundwater channels represent one of the last habitable environments on Mars!

The Survival Challenge: The boxwork ridges form a razor-sharp, jagged labyrinth stretching across 10 to 20 kilometers. For an astronaut in a pressurized spacesuit, traversing this zone carries severe risks: suit puncture hazards from sharp crystalline minerals, elevation dead-ends, and radio signal shadows.

4. Human Factors & Astronaut Safety Mathematics

To differentiate this project from typical submissions, your app should incorporate real NASA Human Research Program (HRP) EVA constraints:

1. Terrain Traversability & Slope Grade Limit

Under Martian gravity (0.38g), a 130 kg suit + human assembly has reduced traction.

  • 0° – 10° Slope: Nominal walking traverse (speed: ~2.5 to 3.5 km/h).
  • 10° – 15° Slope: Caution Zone (metabolic rate spikes by 60%, speed drops to 1.2 km/h).
  • > 15° Slope: NO-GO HAZARD (severe slip risk, soil shear failure, puncture hazard).

2. Metabolic Rate & Oxygen Consumables Formula

The oxygen consumption rate \(\dot{V}_{O_2}\) (liters per hour) can be modeled as a function of speed \(v\) (km/h) and incline gradient \(\theta\) (degrees):

// Dynamic EVA Metabolic Load Calculation
function calculateOxygenBurn(distanceMeters, slopeDegrees, astronautWeightKg = 135) {
  let baseRate = 0.8; // Liters O2 per km on flat terrain (nominal 300 kcal/hr)
  let slopePenalty = 1.0;
  
  if (slopeDegrees > 0) {
    slopePenalty += (slopeDegrees / 15.0) * 1.4; // 140% increase at 15 degree slope
  }
  
  let o2ConsumedLiters = (distanceMeters / 1000.0) * baseRate * slopePenalty;
  return o2ConsumedLiters;
}

3. The "Walkback Horizon" (Abort Rule)

NASA flight rules mandate a 45-minute contingency reserve. The app must dynamically calculate the astronaut's distance from the Habitat or Pressurized Rover. If remaining oxygen or battery equals the energy needed to walk straight back to base plus the 45-minute margin, the system triggers a flashing ABORT EVA / RETURN TO BASE alert.

5. Three Complete Architectural Proposals

Proposal 1: ARES-TAC (Tactical Mission Control Surface Console)

Recommended for Team

Concept: A tactical habitat-based command console modeled after JPL's Maestro and Trek operations tools. It balances science return against crew safety.

  • Frontend: React/Next.js or lightweight HTML5/Tailwind with a dark, telemetry-dense aerospace interface.
  • Map Engine: Leaflet.js rendering NASA Mars Trek WMTS tiles (MOLA shaded relief base + HiRISE local zoom).
  • Active Layers:
    1. Slope Hazard Heatmap: Areas >15° shaded in transparent red.
    2. Science Targets: Waypoints labeled with CRISM mineralogy (Clays, Sulfates, Boxwork Formations).
    3. LOS Shadow: Areas blocked from UHF radio contact with the habitat.
  • Telemetry Bar: Displays real-time estimates of elapsed time, oxygen reserves, metabolic expenditure (kcal/hr), and terrain elevation cross-section.

Proposal 2: HERMES-HUD (Astronaut Wearable Traversal & Safe Pathfinding)

Concept: Designed from the perspective of an astronaut walking inside a pressurized spacesuit looking through a heads-up display.

  • Touch-Optimized UI: Large, high-contrast touch targets suitable for thick suit gloves and gold-tinted visors.
  • Automated Safe Pathfinding: The user selects a target sample rock; the A* routing algorithm automatically plots a traverse avoiding steep slopes and boulder fields.
  • Live "Walkback Horizon": Visual circle showing the maximum safe walking radius before oxygen limits require heading back.

Proposal 3: ISRU-PATHFINDER (Long-Term Survival & Resource Expedition Map)

Concept: Focuses on In-Situ Resource Utilization (ISRU) and colony expansion.

  • Subsurface Ice Mapping: Incorporates SHARAD radar data and NASA SWIM (Subsurface Water Ice Mapping) to locate water ice within 1–2 meters of the surface.
  • Radiation Shielding Analysis: Identifies natural lava tubes and crater rim overhangs that offer protection from Solar Particle Events (SPE).
  • Base Camp Scoring: Clicking any coordinate provides a suitability rating (0–100) based on ice access, slope stability, and solar exposure.

6. NASA Design System & Eliminating "AI Slop"

Judges immediately notice and penalize generic designs with bright purple gradients, vague chatbots, or unrealistic sci-fi fluff. To look like an authentic NASA tool:

  • Color Standards: Deep space carbon background (#0B0E14 or #141822), telemetry cyan (#00F0FF), alert crimson (#EF4444), warning amber (#F59E0B), and nominal green (#10B981).
  • Typography: Use clean technical typefaces: Space Grotesk or Inter for titles, and monospaced JetBrains Mono or Roboto Mono for telemetry values, coordinates, and times.
  • Martian Coordinates: Always use real planetocentric coordinates (e.g., Mount Sharp: 4°35'22" S, 137°26'30" E).
  • Elevation Profile: Include an interactive cross-section graph beneath the map showing the climb from crater floor to the boxwork ridges.
